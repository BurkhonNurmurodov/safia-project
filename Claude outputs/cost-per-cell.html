SAFIA · Repair & maintenance costing standard

Cost per Cell, per Request

Your three buckets describe how the money leaves the company. They cannot describe what a cell costs.

01Verdict

The split is on the wrong axis

Your three types are a procurement classification — they sort spending by how it was purchased. A costing model has to sort spending by what it can be traced to.

Those are different questions, and procurement categories will not roll up into a cost per cell no matter how carefully you maintain them.

Two of the four largest cost lines in a maintenance budget are missing from your model entirely: repairer labour and external services. In food manufacturing, wages and payroll charges are commonly the single biggest line in the maintenance budget — often around half of total spend. Your current model assigns it a weight of zero.

Keep the three buckets. They are useful for purchasing, budgeting and inventory control, and Finance already understands them. Just stop asking them to answer a question they are not shaped to answer, and build the cost model as a second layer on top.

Your starting position

A store exists but withdrawals are not recorded against a job. Repairer time is not recorded — only the request's opened / started / closed timestamps. Parts bought per-job go to Finance as a purchase request.

That means none of the four direct cost lines is currently captured in a usable form. The rollout in §06 is ordered to close that gap by value, cheapest fix first.

02Diagnosis

Why the three buckets can't roll up

  1. Purchase date is not consumption date. Type 2 materials hit the books when bought, not when used. A month with a large bulk purchase looks expensive even if nothing broke; a month of heavy breakdowns looks cheap because the store was already full. The monthly figure tracks your purchasing calendar, not your reliability.
  2. None of the buckets carries a cost object. A cost line is only useful if it names a cell or a request. Type 1 is the only one that could — the purchase request presumably names a machine — and even that link is not currently recorded as a field you can group by.
  3. Type 3 is not a type of repair. "They just tighten something" describes what the job consumed (nothing), not a category of cost. Meanwhile the wrench used on that job is the same wrench used on a type 1 job. By naming type 3 after tools, you have accidentally charged all tool cost to the cheapest jobs in the plant.
  4. Labour and contractors are absent. Man-hours, overtime, standby time, and outside welding, calibration or vendor service visits appear in none of the three. Publish a per-cell cost without them and the number is not merely low — it is unevenly low. A cell with many slow, cheap repairs looks better than a cell with one expensive part swap, which inverts the true picture and points improvement effort at the wrong cell.
03The model

Sort by traceability, not by purchase pattern

Fix two cost objects and one rule. The cost objects are the repair request and the production cell. The rule is that every cost is either direct — you can name the request it belongs to — or pooled — you cannot, so it is spread to cells by a driver.

Direct to a request

Costs that can name their ticket

D1 · LABOUR
Man-hours × loaded hourly rateUsually the largest line. Headcount × hours, not elapsed time.
D2 · SPARE PARTS
Parts bought for that job, or issued from store against itYour type 1, plus the tracked part of type 2.
D3 · MATERIALS
Store issues above the materiality thresholdValued at moving average cost on issue, not on purchase.
D4 · EXTERNAL SERVICES
Contractor, welding shop, calibration, vendor visitCurrently missing from your model.

Pooled, then allocated

Real costs that no ticket can claim

P1 · CONSUMABLES
Bolts, tape, grease, rags, lubricant spraysBelow the threshold. Never worth chasing to a job.
P2 · TOOLS & EQUIPMENT
Amortisation over expected lifeYour type 3, spread across every job — not only the ones with no parts.
P3 · DEPARTMENT OVERHEAD
Supervision, planning, workshop, transport, PPE, training, standby time
P4 · STORE HANDLING
Storekeeper time, obsolescence, inventory write-offs

Consequential — report beside, never inside

The cost of the stoppage, not the cost of the repair

C1 · DOWNTIME
Stopped minutes × contribution margin per production minuteUsually as large as the repair itself, often larger. Mixing it into repair cost makes both numbers unusable — keep them in adjacent columns.

The mechanism that makes this cheap

Do not run a separate allocation exercise for P1–P4. Bury the whole pool inside the loaded hourly rate. Then one rate carries labour, overhead, tools and consumables together, and the pool self-allocates to cells in proportion to where repairers actually spend their hours — which is the best driver available to you and requires no extra data collection.

Cost of a request = hours × loaded rate + parts + materials + services Cost of a cell = Σ its requests + its share of any residual pool

On materiality

Set a value threshold so that roughly the top 20% of store items by value — which will cover about 80% of store value — require a request number on issue. Everything below is issued freely into the consumables pool. Chasing a bolt to a work order costs more in clerk time than the bolt.

05The rate

Building the loaded hourly rate

One number does most of the work, and one mistake in it invalidates everything downstream: the divisor. Divide by productive hours — wrench time — not by paid hours. Wrench time in most plants runs between half and two thirds of paid hours, the rest going to travel, waiting for parts, briefings and breaks. Measure yours; until you have, start at 55% and refine.

wages + payroll taxes + supervision + workshop & utilities + tool amortisation + untracked consumables + transport + PPE + training productive (wrench) hours = loaded hourly rate, recalculated quarterly and applied forward

Fix the rate for a quarter and apply it prospectively. Do not restate closed months when the new rate lands — a cost report that keeps rewriting its own history loses the trust of the people using it.

Under the three-bucket model, this request attributes zero UZS to cell 8281.

The bearing came out of the monthly materials purchase — already expensed, with no issue record naming the job. The labour exists in no bucket. The failure is invisible in the cost report, and cell 8281 looks cheap to run.

07Rollout

Six phases, about six months

Sequenced by value per unit of effort, given what you can and cannot capture today. Every phase has a gate — do not begin the next one until it is met, because a cost report published on thin data destroys its own credibility permanently.

Phase 0Weeks 1–2

Write the policy on one page

Agree the two cost objects, the four direct lines, the contents of the pool, the materiality threshold for store issues, and the capitalisation threshold for tools. Finance, the repair department head and production sign it.

This is where costing programmes die when skipped. Without a signed definition, every month becomes an argument about what counts.

Gate — one signed page, three signatures
Phase 1Weeks 3–6

Capture man-hours at request close

Two fields, filled by the repairer when closing the request: how many people, and how many hours. A rounded half-hour self-report captures most of the available value at almost no cost.

Do not derive hours from the timestamps. Closed minus opened is elapsed time — it includes waiting for a part, the night shift, and lunch. Using it as labour systematically overstates the slow-but-cheap jobs, which are exactly the ones you would then wrongly prioritise.

If the department resists the fields, run a one-month time study instead: sample actual man-hours on roughly 100 requests across categories, derive average hours per request per category, apply to the full population. Accuracy around ±20%, and it buys you time to win the argument.

Gate — over 80% of closed requests carry hours and headcount
Phase 2Weeks 3–8
parallel

Put a request number on every issue and invoice

The store already exists — the missing piece is a four-field withdrawal record: date, item, quantity, request number. Paper slips are fine to start. One rule enforces it: no issue without a request number.

Same field on the Finance purchase request form, and on every contractor act before it is paid. Accounting changes at the same time: materials capitalise to inventory on purchase and expense on issue.

Gate — over 80% of above-threshold issues name a request
Phase 3Weeks 5–8
parallel

Finance builds rate v1

Assemble the pool from the last twelve months of the general ledger. Estimate wrench hours. Publish the rate, its components and its effective quarter, so anyone reading a cost can see what is inside it.

Gate — rate published with its build-up visible
Phase 4Weeks 9–12

First close, with the gap shown

Produce month one of the per-cell report and reconcile it to the ledger. Expect 60–75% coverage in the first month — that is normal. Publish the coverage figure on the same page as the costs. A number with a visible gap is useful; a number with a hidden gap is dangerous.

Gate — reconciliation runs, coverage stated on the report
Phase 5Months 4–6

Normalise and segment, then add downtime

Absolute cost per cell is misleading — a large cell should cost more. Add cost per 1 000 units produced, or per production hour, and repair cost as a percentage of the cell's replacement asset value.

Then split by work type: breakdown, corrective, preventive, improvement. This is the split that makes the report actionable — high preventive cost on a cell is healthy, high breakdown cost is bleeding, and only this column tells them apart. Finally overlay downtime cost, and you have the total cost of unreliability per cell, which is what justifies capital.

Gate — every cell cost carries a denominator and a work-type split
08Controls

Two controls keep the numbers honest

Reconciliation to the ledger

Every period, the costs posted to requests plus the pool must equal the repair department's total ledger spend, within about 2%.

Σ costs on requests + Σ pool = repair department ledger spend ± 2%

Without this, the per-cell report becomes a parallel set of books that drifts away from the P&L, and Finance will correctly stop trusting it. Target traceable coverage of 90% by month six and 95% by month twelve.

A published unattributed rate

Report, on the same page as the costs, the share of requests missing a cell code, missing hours, or missing a parts link. The credibility of the whole report rests on the gap being visible rather than discovered later by someone else.

What to report per cell, monthly

  • Repair cost, split into labour / parts & materials / services
  • The same cost per 1 000 units produced, and as a percentage of replacement asset value
  • Split by breakdown, corrective, preventive, improvement
  • Number of requests, mean time to repair, mean time between failures
  • Downtime cost, in its own column
  • Coverage: percentage of ledger spend traced to a request

Repair cost as a share of replacement asset value is the usual cross-plant yardstick — figures around 3–6% are commonly cited for food and beverage plants, with the best performers nearer 2–3%. Treat any published benchmark as a direction of travel, not a target, until you have two or three of your own years to compare against.

09Traps

What goes wrong

Elapsed time as labourClosed minus opened includes waiting, shifts and breaks. It inflates exactly the jobs that were cheapest in man-hours.
Paid hours as the divisorUnderstates the rate by roughly 40–45%. The missing cost lands on no cell at all.
Chasing cheap consumablesTracking a bolt to a request costs more clerk time than the bolt. Set the threshold and defend it.
No reconciliationThe report silently diverges from the ledger and becomes a second, untrusted set of books.
Tools charged to today's cellA drill bought during a repair on one cell is not that cell's cost. It belongs in the pool, spread over its life.
Comparing cells by absolute costWithout a denominator, the biggest cell always looks worst and the improvement effort goes to the wrong place.
Publishing too earlyOne visibly wrong number in month one, and the department will argue about the method instead of the machines for a year.
Downtime folded into repair costMerging them hides both. Repair cost drives maintenance decisions; downtime cost drives capital decisions.
